<h3>Speaker &amp; Title</h3>

<p>Jake Green, "Leaders We Deserve, Candidates We Lack: Descriptive and Substantive Representation of American Youth"</p>

<h3>Abstract</h3>

<p>Young adults are substantially underrepresented in American elected office, but the source of that gap is not straightforward. Does age itself depress voter support for younger candidates, or do barriers elsewhere in the political pipeline limit the number of young people who enter, advance through, and remain in elective office? I combine semi-structured interviews with young political actors, two conjoint survey experiments, and an observational analysis of age representation across U.S. state legislative chambers. In one sample, younger candidate age does not reduce vote choice overall, but respondents judge younger candidates as less likely to win and to succeed in office. Prior elected experience, by contrast, produces large positive shifts in vote choice, perceived electability, and perceived governing success. A student sample shows a strong preference for younger candidates while remaining pessimistic about their electoral prospects. Interviews identify barriers to entry, including financial constraints, party gatekeeping, limited campaign knowledge, and the importance of recruitment. State legislative evidence points in the same direction: young adults are substantially less represented in upper than lower chambers, and youth representation is positively associated with legislative compensation relative to typical earnings in the state. Together, the findings are more consistent with a political-pipeline account than one of simple voter-hostility. They suggest that age disparities in office can persist even when voters are willing to support young candidates, for political experience, perceived viability, and the economic sustainability of political service remain unevenly distributed.<br>&nbsp;</p>
